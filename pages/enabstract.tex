% English Abstract

Hardware encoders such as NVENC have become the default choice for live streaming and cloud gaming thanks to their very low latency and high throughput, yet once the target bitrate is pushed down, the reconstructed frames quickly suffer from blocking, ringing, and texture blur. Most deep restoration networks proposed to alleviate these artefacts rely on tens of millions of parameters and hundreds of milliseconds of inference time, which keeps them out of a real hardware encoding pipeline. This engineering gap is the starting point of the present thesis.

This work builds a lightweight co-processing scheme around an NVENC encoding link. The encoder itself is kept untouched and treated as a black-box environment, while a 113K-parameter Rate-Adaptive Pre-processing Network (RAPNet) is inserted before it. Within an Actor-Critic framework, RAPNet outputs a residual correction to the input frame, and a compound reward computed from the relative rate-distortion gain against a fixed Baseline lets the policy trade bitrate against perceptual quality on the fly. To curb the chroma-channel collapse and checkerboard artefacts observed during early training, a frequency-aware composite loss is further introduced, combining a weighted MSE term, a non-edge high-frequency suppression term, and a total-variation smoothing term.

Experiments are carried out on five UVG 1080p YUV420 sequences at QP$\in\{37,40,42,45,47\}$. RAPNet adds 5.4~ms per frame, roughly 12\% of the end-to-end latency, and in exchange achieves an average BD-LPIPS of $-9.48\%$; the gains on ParkScene and BasketballDrive reach $-13.45\%$ and $-12.84\%$ respectively. These results indicate that, without modifying the hardware encoder itself, a lightweight reinforcement-learning preprocessor can be attached to a real NVENC pipeline and still deliver a measurable perceptual rate-distortion improvement at millisecond-level overhead.

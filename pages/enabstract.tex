% English Abstract

Hardware encoders such as NVENC are widely used in live streaming and cloud gaming because they offer very low latency and high throughput. However, once the target bitrate is pushed down, the reconstructed frames quickly suffer from blocking, ringing, and texture blur. Most deep restoration networks proposed to alleviate these artefacts rely on tens of millions of parameters and hundreds of milliseconds of inference time, which keeps them out of a practical hardware encoding pipeline. This engineering gap is the starting point of the present thesis.

This work builds a lightweight co-processing scheme around an NVENC encoding link. The encoder itself is kept untouched and treated as a black-box environment, while a 113K-parameter Rate-Adaptive Pre-processing Network (RAPNet) is inserted before it. Within an Actor-Critic framework, RAPNet outputs a residual correction to the input frame, and a compound reward computed from the relative rate-distortion gain against a fixed Baseline lets the policy trade bitrate against perceptual quality on the fly. To curb the chroma-channel collapse and checkerboard artefacts observed during early training, a frequency-aware composite loss is further introduced, combining a weighted MSE term, a non-edge high-frequency suppression term, and a total-variation smoothing term.

Experiments are carried out on five UVG 1080p YUV420 sequences, using five low-bitrate QP settings: 37, 40, 42, 45, and 47. RAPNet adds 5.4 ms per frame, roughly 12\% of the end-to-end latency, and in exchange achieves an average BD-LPIPS of $-9.48\%$; the gains on ParkScene and BasketballDrive reach $-13.45\%$ and $-12.84\%$ respectively. These results indicate that a lightweight reinforcement-learning preprocessor can be attached to a real NVENC pipeline and still improve perceptual rate-distortion performance with only millisecond-level overhead.

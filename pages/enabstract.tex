% English Abstract

Hardware encoders such as NVENC are widely used in live streaming and cloud gaming because they provide low latency and high throughput. Under low-bitrate settings, however, their reconstructed frames often suffer from blocking, ringing, and texture blur. Existing deep restoration networks are usually too large and too slow to be directly inserted into a real-time hardware encoding pipeline. This thesis therefore studies whether a lightweight preprocessor can improve perceptual rate-distortion performance without modifying the hardware encoder itself.

This work proposes a reinforcement-learning preprocessing method for low-bitrate NVENC video coding. The encoder itself is kept untouched and treated as a black-box environment, while a 113K-parameter Rate-Adaptive Pre-processing Network (RAPNet) is inserted before it. Within an Actor-Critic framework, RAPNet outputs a residual correction to the input frame, and a compound reward computed from the relative rate-distortion gain against a fixed Baseline guides the policy to balance bitrate saving and perceptual quality. To curb the chroma-channel collapse and checkerboard artefacts observed during early training, a frequency-aware composite loss is further introduced, combining a weighted MSE term, a non-edge high-frequency suppression term, and a total-variation smoothing term.

Experiments use five HEVC CTC Class B 1080p YUV420 sequences as source data. During training and evaluation, $960\times540$ coding clips are constructed by random cropping, and five low-bitrate QP settings are tested: 37, 40, 42, 45, and 47. RAPNet adds 5.4 ms per frame, roughly 12\% of the end-to-end latency, and achieves an average BD-LPIPS of $-9.48\%$; the gains on ParkScene and BasketballDrive reach $-13.45\%$ and $-12.84\%$ respectively. These results show that, in the evaluated NVENC pipeline, a lightweight reinforcement-learning preprocessor can improve perceptual rate-distortion performance with limited additional latency.
